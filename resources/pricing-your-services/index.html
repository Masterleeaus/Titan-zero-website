Practical guide

How to price a service job without guessing

Build a price from actual labour, materials, travel, overhead and the time the job consumes.

Start with the job, not a competitor’s price

Record the work included, likely duration, travel, materials, disposal and any return visit that is part of the scope. A competitor’s advertised rate may describe a different job, area or service level.

Use a recent completed job as a starting point. Separate direct job costs from monthly overhead so the same cost is not counted twice.

Calculate direct cost and contribution

Add labour at a realistic loaded rate, materials, vehicle or travel cost, equipment and other job-specific expenses. Subtract that total from the price to see contribution before overhead.

A markup and a margin are different calculations. Check which one you are using before setting a target; the job-cost sheet shows the resulting contribution and margin together.

Check the monthly volume

A price that covers a job’s direct cost can still fail to cover rent, insurance, software, vehicles and other fixed expenses. Use the break-even calculator to estimate the job count needed to cover those costs.

Then compare the required volume with the team’s productive hours and normal travel pattern. If the volume does not fit, revisit scope, pricing, cost or capacity assumptions.

Keep the estimate honest

Treat starter values as prompts, not market prices. Validate them with current supplier quotes, payroll records and the jobs you actually deliver.

The free business.gov.au financial templates can help structure a cash-flow or budget view. For tax and employment questions, check the relevant Australian Government guidance.

This guide is general information for planning a business workflow. It is not tax, legal, safety or employment advice.
